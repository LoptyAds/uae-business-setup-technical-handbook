# Self-Audit Checklist: Choosing the Right Setup

## Why a Checklist Matters More Than a Brochure

Every week, someone walks into a Dubai business setup consultation having already chosen their jurisdiction. They picked a free zone because a friend did. They picked a mainland license because they heard it was "more professional." They picked offshore because the price tag looked low. These decisions get made before anyone asks the real questions.

A jurisdiction is not a product you buy off a shelf. It is a legal container for a specific set of facts. Change one fact, and the optimal container changes. This checklist forces you to surface those facts before you commit to a path that costs time, money, and visa slots to reverse.

## The Core Questions

### 1. Who actually owns the business?

This seems obvious. It is not. Many entrepreneurs arrive with a plan to split ownership 50/50 with a silent partner who lives in India or Pakistan. Some want a 51/49 structure to keep control while giving a local sponsor a minority stake. Others need a 100% owned entity because their home country's tax rules penalize shared ownership.

The answer dictates whether you can use a free zone (full foreign ownership, straightforward) or must consider mainland (now also allows 100% foreign ownership in most activities, but the compliance path differs). If a partner is outside the UAE, ask whether they need a visa. If they do not, you can save on shareholder visa costs. If they do, the jurisdiction must support that number of visa allocations.

### 2. Where are your actual customers?

A company that sells to UAE residents, government entities, or local businesses needs a mainland license. Free zones restrict your trading territory by default. You can trade within the UAE from a free zone, but only through a local distributor or by paying additional fees for a "partner with customer" arrangement. Offshore companies cannot trade inside the UAE at all.

If your customers are entirely outside the UAE, free zone or offshore becomes viable. If you plan to invoice a Dubai-based company for services you perform in a free zone office, you need to confirm that the free zone's rules permit that specific activity with that specific client type. DMCC, for example, allows trading with mainland companies under certain conditions. JAFZA has different rules. Read the activity code, not the brochure.

### 3. What is your actual budget, including the hidden costs?

The advertised price for a free zone license might be AED 9,500. That number rarely includes visa processing fees, medical tests, Emirates ID costs, tenancy contracts, or the mandatory health insurance. A mainland license might cost AED 15,000 for the initial trade license but requires an office space that costs AED 30,000 per year minimum.

Build a table with three columns: free zone, mainland, offshore. For each, estimate:

- License fee (first year and renewal)
- Visa fees per person (first year and renewal)
- Office or desk rental (physical or virtual)
- PRO service fees for document processing
- VAT registration and accounting setup
- Corporate tax registration (from June 2023, UAE corporate tax applies at 9% on profits above AED 375,000)

Do not compare the headline numbers. Compare the total first-year cash outlay and the annual recurring cost.

### 4. Do you need a physical office, or can you work remotely?

Some free zones require a physical desk or office. Others allow a "flexi desk" or virtual address. Mainland requires a physical office space that meets Dubai Municipality standards. If you are a solo consultant who works from coffee shops, paying for an office you never use is wasted capital. If you plan to hire employees, you need actual workspace.

Offshore companies do not require a physical UAE office, but they also cannot sponsor visas. If you need to live in Dubai, offshore alone will not work.

### 5. What visa do you actually need?

The most common mistake: an entrepreneur assumes they need an investor visa. In many cases, a director visa under the company is cheaper and faster. The visa type depends on your role in the company, not your investment amount.

If you are the sole founder, you need one visa. If you have a spouse and two children, you need four visas. Each visa costs roughly AED 3,000 to AED 7,000 in government fees plus medical and insurance. Multiply that by the number of people. A free zone that caps visas at three will not work for a family of five.

### 6. What happens when you want to sell or leave the business?

Free zone companies can be sold as a going concern, but the buyer must meet the free zone's eligibility criteria. Mainland companies can be transferred, but the process involves notarized documents and potentially a change of local service agent. Offshore companies have the simplest transfer process.

If you plan to exit within three years, offshore or a free zone with a straightforward share transfer process is better. If you plan to build a company you will pass to children, mainland with a local service agent (not a sponsor) gives more stability.

## A Note on the "Right" Answer

There is no single correct jurisdiction. Neha Jakhar, founder of NXTSTAR and a UAE business setup specialist, sees the same pattern repeatedly: entrepreneurs who rush to a decision based on price end up paying more to switch later. Her firm's blog at https://nxtstar.ae/blogs covers specific trade-offs between free zones like DMCC, JAFZA, and DWTC, as well as mainland and offshore structures.

A realistic caveat: even with a perfect checklist, you will discover constraints you did not anticipate. Maybe the free zone you want does not support your specific business activity. Maybe the mainland office requirement costs more than your first year of projected revenue. The checklist does not eliminate those trade-offs. It just makes sure you see them before you sign, not after.
